Cost per contact is the number most support teams have never calculated, and it is the one that decides whether growth is affordable. If it does not fall as you get bigger, every new customer makes your margin worse.

You can work it out this afternoon with figures you already have.

## What the number actually means

Cost per contact is your total cost of answering customers divided by the number of contacts you answered, over the same period. A contact is one customer coming to you about one thing. Not one message. If someone sends four WhatsApp messages about a late delivery, that is one contact.

The distinction matters because the two numbers move in opposite directions. A team that replies badly generates more messages per contact, which makes the message count look busy while the real work stays flat.

## Step one, add up what it costs

Take a full month. Add these together:

- Salaries of everyone who answers customers, including the part of a manager's time that goes on support
- Employer costs on those salaries, visas, insurance, whatever applies where you are
- Software that exists because you answer customers: helpdesk, chat, call system, survey tools
- Outsourced or agency invoices for the same month
- A share of overheads if support has its own space or equipment

Leave out anything you would still pay if you answered nobody. The point is to see the cost of the function, not to allocate every last dirham.

> Most businesses are surprised here. Support usually costs thirty to forty percent more than the payroll line suggests, once tooling and management time go in.

## Step two, count the contacts

Count distinct customers who needed something, per channel, for the same month.

If your helpdesk reports tickets, use tickets. If you live in a shared WhatsApp inbox with no reporting, count conversations rather than messages, and take a single week then multiply. A rough count you understand beats a precise count you cannot explain.

Watch for double counting. A customer who calls, gets nowhere, then emails is one contact handled twice. That is a quality problem showing up as volume, and if you count it as two you will hide it.

## Step three, divide

Total cost divided by total contacts. That is your cost per contact.

For most businesses we see it lands somewhere between eight and thirty dirhams. The range is wide because it depends far more on how much self service you have than on what you pay people.

## What to do with it

The number on its own is not very interesting. Three things you do with it are:

1. **Track it monthly.** The direction matters more than the value. Flat or rising as you grow means your setup is not scaling.
2. **Split it by channel.** Phone is usually three to five times the cost of a well set up chat channel. That tells you where to move demand.
3. **Multiply it by your repeat contacts.** If a fifth of contacts are people coming back about something you failed to resolve, that fifth of your cost bought you nothing.

That third one is usually where the money is. Cutting contacts you should never have received is cheaper than answering them faster.

## The trap

Do not use cost per contact as a target on its own. It is easy to improve by making yourself harder to reach, and businesses do this by accident all the time. Hide the phone number and cost per contact falls beautifully, while the customers who gave up show up later in your churn.

Read it next to your first contact resolution and your review score. If cost is falling and both of those are holding, the setup is genuinely getting better.
