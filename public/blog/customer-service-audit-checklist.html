Customer service audit checklist

25 questions to find what your customer service is really costing you. Score your team in about an hour, using numbers you already have.

A customer service audit is a close look at how your support really runs. It covers how much work comes in, how fast and how well it gets handled, and what it costs. The 25 questions below are the ones we start with. You can answer most of them in about an hour, using numbers you already have.

Score each question honestly. Yes = 2 points, partly = 1, no = 0. The total is out of 50, and the scoring guide is at the end.

1. What comes in

These questions show whether you understand your demand. Most support problems start here, not with the team.

  1. Do you know how many contacts you handled last month, across every channel? Email, phone, WhatsApp, chat and social all count.
  2. Do you know your top five reasons for contact? Not a guess. Tagged or counted from real conversations.
  3. Do you know your contact rate? That is contacts divided by orders or customers. It tells you if demand grows faster than the business.
  4. Can you see which contacts come back? A customer writing again about the same problem is a sign the first answer didn't fix it.
  5. Does someone outside support act on the top reasons? If "where is my order?" is your biggest reason, the fix sits with delivery, not with the team answering.

2. Speed

Speed is what customers notice first. But fixing it alone can get expensive, as we explain in why reply time is not the real problem.

  1. Do you have a target first reply time for each channel? Written down and known by the team.
  2. Do you meet it at least 90% of the time?
  3. Do you know your reply time at weekends and in the evening? This is where most businesses go quiet.
  4. Do you know how long it takes to fully solve an issue? Not just to reply.
  5. Do urgent issues, such as payment failures or complaints, get handled first?

3. Quality

  1. Do you measure customer satisfaction after contacts? Even a simple one-question survey counts.
  2. Do you review a sample of conversations each month? Reading real chats finds problems no report will show.
  3. Is there one written answer for each common question? So two agents never give two different answers.
  4. Can the person answering see the customer's order or account? If not, customers repeat themselves in every contact.
  5. Is there a clear path for complaints, with an owner and a deadline?

4. Cost and team

  1. Do you know your cost per contact? If not, here is how to calculate cost per contact in about twenty minutes.
  2. Has cost per contact gone down as you grew? If it stays flat or rises, every new customer makes your margin worse.
  3. Is your team the right size for when contacts actually arrive? Too many people at 3pm and too few at 9am is common.
  4. Has staff turnover been under 30% in the last year? High turnover means you keep paying to train people who then leave.
  5. Can you handle a busy month, such as a sale or a delivery problem, without everyone working late?

5. Tools and data

  1. Do all channels land in one place? Or does the team check an inbox, a phone and WhatsApp separately?
  2. Does every contact get a reason tag? Without tags, question 2 above is guesswork.
  3. Can you pull last month's numbers in under ten minutes?
  4. Is your chatbot or self-service measured on problems solved, not chats started? A bot that hands every chat to a person is a cost, not a saving.
  5. Does a manager look at the numbers every week and act on them?

What your score means

Score What it usually means
40 to 50 Your support is well run. Look for small gains in cost per contact.
25 to 39 The basics work, but money is leaking. Usually repeat contacts and poor staffing patterns.
Under 25 The team is working hard on problems that shouldn't exist. The fix is in the first and fourth sections.

The pattern in the example above is the one we see most often. Speed looks fine, so everyone thinks support is fine. But nobody knows why customers get in touch, and nobody knows what each contact costs. That combination means costs rise with every new customer.

We saw exactly this with a B2B payroll team. Requests went back and forth between support and operations by email, and nothing was measured. Nineteen business clients left in ten months before anyone could say why. After a rebuild that gave support end-to-end ownership and added proper tracking, incoming requests fell by 45%. First response targets were met 98% of the time, up from 75%.

What to do with your score

Start with your lowest section, not your highest. In most businesses that is section 1 or section 4. Pick the three "no" answers that would be easiest to turn into a "yes" this month, and give each one an owner.

If you want a formal standard to compare against, ISO 18295-1 sets out requirements for customer contact centres. It is written for larger operations, but its headings are a useful cross-check.

Frequently asked questions

What is a customer service audit?

A close review of how your support runs. It covers what comes in, how fast and well it is handled, what it costs, and the tools you rely on. The goal is to find where money and customers are being lost.

How long does a customer service audit take?

A self-check with this list takes about an hour. A full audit with real data, conversation reviews and cost analysis usually takes one to three weeks.

How often should you audit customer service?

Once a year as a full review, and after any big change, such as a new channel, a new tool, or fast growth.

What is the most common problem an audit finds?

Not knowing why customers get in touch. Without contact reasons, businesses fix speed by hiring, and costs rise with every new customer.
